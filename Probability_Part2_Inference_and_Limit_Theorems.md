## 1. Introduction to Bayesian Inference

### 1.1 The Big Picture

Probability theory and inference are two sides of the same coin. In probability theory (analysis), we start with a known model and compute probabilities of various events. In **inference** (or statistics), the direction reverses: we observe data and try to figure out which model generated it.

This arises everywhere: polling and elections, signal processing and communications, finance, recommendation systems, medical diagnosis, tracking objects, climate modeling, interpreting physics experiments, and GPS positioning. The common thread is that we have an **unknown quantity** we want to learn about and **noisy or incomplete observations** that carry information about it.

### 1.2 Hypothesis Testing vs. Estimation

Inference problems fall into two broad categories.

In **hypothesis testing**, the unknown takes one of a small number of possible values (alternative hypotheses). The goal is to decide which one is correct and to keep the probability of an incorrect decision small. A classic example: is the object on the radar screen an airplane or a bird?

In **estimation**, the unknown is a numerical quantity (possibly continuous). The goal is to produce an estimate that is "close" to the true value. For instance: what is the true bias of a coin, given the results of many tosses?

### 1.3 The Bayesian Inference Framework

In the Bayesian approach, the unknown $`\Theta`$ is modeled as a **random variable** with a **prior distribution** $`p_\Theta`$ or $`f_\Theta`$ that reflects our initial beliefs before seeing any data. We then observe $`X`$, which is generated by the **observation model** $`p_{X \mid \Theta}`$ or $`f_{X \mid \Theta}`$.

The complete answer to any Bayesian inference problem is the **posterior distribution** of $`\Theta`$ given $`X = x`$, obtained via the appropriate version of Bayes' rule. The posterior captures everything we know about $`\Theta`$ after incorporating the observed data.

### 1.4 Four Versions of the Bayes Rule

Depending on whether $`\Theta`$ and $`X`$ are discrete or continuous, there are four cases.

**Discrete $`\Theta`$, discrete $`X`$:**

$$p_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p_\Theta(\theta) \cdot p_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)}{p_X(x)}$$

where $`p_X(x) = \sum_{\theta'} p_\Theta(\theta') \cdot p_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta')`$.

**Discrete $`\Theta`$, continuous $`X`$:**

$$p_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p_\Theta(\theta) \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)}{f_X(x)}$$

where $`f_X(x) = \sum_{\theta'} p_\Theta(\theta') \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta')`$.

**Continuous $`\Theta`$, discrete $`X`$:**

$$f_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) = \frac{f_\Theta(\theta) \cdot p_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)}{p_X(x)}$$

where $`p_X(x) = \int f_\Theta(\theta') \cdot p_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta') \, d\theta'`$.

**Continuous $`\Theta`$, continuous $`X`$:**

$$f_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) = \frac{f_\Theta(\theta) \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)}{f_X(x)}$$

where $`f_X(x) = \int f_\Theta(\theta') \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta') \, d\theta'`$.

In every case, the structure is the same: **posterior** $`\propto`$ **prior** $`\times`$ **likelihood**.

### 1.5 Point Estimates

The posterior distribution is the full answer, but we often want a single "best guess" for $`\Theta`$. Two standard choices:

**Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate.** Choose the value of $`\theta`$ that maximizes the posterior:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \, p_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) \quad \text{(discrete)} \qquad \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \, f_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) \quad \text{(continuous)}$$

The MAP estimate is the single most probable value of $`\Theta`$ given the data.

**Least Mean Squares (LMS) estimate.** Choose the conditional expectation:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{LMS}} = E[\Theta \mid X = x]$$

This minimizes the mean squared error $`E[(\Theta - \hat{\theta})^2 \mid X = x]`$ (see Section 3).

### 1.6 Performance Measures

For **hypothesis testing** (discrete $`\Theta`$), the natural measure is the **probability of error**:

$$P(\hat{\theta} \ne \Theta \mid X = x) \quad \text{(conditional on a specific observation)}$$

$$P(\hat{\theta} \ne \Theta) \quad \text{(overall, averaged over all possible observations)}$$

The MAP rule minimizes both the conditional and the overall probability of error.

For **estimation** (continuous $`\Theta`$), the natural measure is the **mean squared error (MSE)**:

$$E[(\hat{\theta} - \Theta)^2 \mid X = x] \quad \text{(conditional)} \qquad E[(\hat{\theta} - \Theta)^2] \quad \text{(overall)}$$

### 1.7 Example: Discrete Hypothesis Testing

Suppose $`\Theta`$ takes values $`\lbrace 1, 2, 3 \rbrace`$ with prior probabilities 0.6, 0.3, 0.1. We observe $`X = \Theta + W`$ where $`W \sim N(0, \sigma^2)`$ is independent noise. Then $`f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta) = f_W(x - \theta)`$.

The MAP rule selects the hypothesis $`\theta`$ that maximizes $`p_\Theta(\theta) \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)`$. This is essentially "which hypothesis makes the data most likely, weighted by the prior?"

### 1.8 Example: Inferring the Bias of a Coin (Beta Distribution)

This is the canonical Bayesian estimation example. Let $`\Theta`$ be the unknown bias of a coin with a uniform prior on $`[0,1]`$ (i.e., $`f_\Theta(\theta) = 1`$). We toss the coin $`n`$ times and observe $`K`$ heads.

The likelihood is binomial: $`p_{K \mid \Theta}(k \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{k} \theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}`$.

The posterior is:

$$f_{\Theta \mid K}(\theta \mid k) = \frac{\theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}}{d(n,k)}$$

where $`d(n,k)`$ is a normalizing constant. This is a **Beta distribution** with parameters $`(k+1, n-k+1)`$.

**Point estimates:**

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{k}{n}, \qquad \hat{\theta}_{\text{LMS}} = E[\Theta \mid K = k] = \frac{k+1}{n+2}$$

The MAP estimate is identical to the classical "fraction of heads." The LMS estimate is slightly pulled toward 1/2 (a form of **shrinkage**), which is especially noticeable when $`n`$ is small. As $`n \to \infty`$, both estimates converge to $`k/n`$.

**If the prior is also Beta** with parameters $`(\alpha, \beta)`$, i.e., $`f_\Theta(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}`$, then the posterior is $`\text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)`$. The Beta family is **conjugate** to the binomial likelihood, meaning the posterior stays in the same family as the prior.

## 2. Linear Models with Normal Noise

### 2.1 Why This Model Matters

The linear-normal model is the workhorse of Bayesian inference because it leads to closed-form solutions with elegant properties. The setup is: the unknown $`\Theta`$ and the noise $`W`$ are both normally distributed, and the observation is a linear function of $`\Theta`$ plus noise.

### 2.2 Recognizing Normal PDFs

A key computational tool: any expression of the form $`c \cdot \exp(-\alpha x^2 + \beta x + \gamma)`$ with $`\alpha > 0`$ is proportional to a normal PDF with mean $`\beta/(2\alpha)`$ and variance $`1/(2\alpha)`$. This allows us to read off the posterior parameters without completing the full Bayes' rule calculation.

### 2.3 Estimating a Normal Random Variable in Additive Normal Noise

The simplest case: $`X = \Theta + W`$ where $`\Theta \sim N(0, 1)`$ and $`W \sim N(0, 1)`$ are independent. Then the posterior $`f_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x)`$ is proportional to:

$$\exp\left(-\frac{\theta^2}{2}\right) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \theta)^2}{2}\right) = \exp\left(-\theta^2 + \theta x - \frac{x^2}{2}\right)$$

Completing the square in $`\theta`$, the posterior is $`N(x/2, 1/2)`$. Both the MAP and LMS estimates equal $`x/2`$: the estimate is halfway between the prior mean (0) and the observation ($`x`$).

### 2.4 General Case: Single Observation

More generally, let $`\Theta \sim N(x_0, \sigma_0^2)`$ and $`W \sim N(0, \sigma_1^2)`$, independent, with $`X = \Theta + W`$. Then the posterior is normal, and:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \hat{\theta}_{\text{LMS}} = E[\Theta \mid X = x] = \frac{\sigma_1^2 x_0 + \sigma_0^2 x}{\sigma_0^2 + \sigma_1^2}$$

This is a **weighted average** of the prior mean $`x_0`$ and the observation $`x`$, where the weights are inversely proportional to the respective variances. A more precise prior (small $`\sigma_0^2`$) pulls the estimate toward $`x_0`$; a more precise observation (small $`\sigma_1^2`$) pulls it toward $`x`$.

The posterior variance is:

$$\text{var}(\Theta \mid X = x) = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}}$$

This is always smaller than both $`\sigma_0^2`$ and $`\sigma_1^2`$: observing data always reduces uncertainty.

### 2.5 Multiple Observations

Suppose $`\Theta \sim N(x_0, \sigma_0^2)`$ and we observe $`X_i = \Theta + W_i`$ for $`i = 1, \ldots, n`$, where the $`W_i \sim N(0, \sigma_i^2)`$ are independent of each other and of $`\Theta`$.

The posterior of $`\Theta`$ given $`X_1 = x_1, \ldots, X_n = x_n`$ is still normal. The MAP and LMS estimates coincide and equal:

$$\hat{\theta} = E[\Theta \mid X = x] = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n} x_i / \sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=0}^{n} 1/\sigma_i^2}$$

The estimate is a weighted average of the prior mean and all observations, each weighted by the reciprocal of its variance (i.e., its **precision**). More precise measurements carry more weight.

The posterior variance (conditional MSE) is:

$$\text{var}(\Theta \mid X = x) = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{n} 1/\sigma_i^2}$$

When all observation variances are equal ($`\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2`$ for $`i \ge 1`$), this simplifies to $`\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1}`$, which decreases as $`n`$ grows. More data means less uncertainty.

### 2.6 Key Properties of Linear Normal Models

In the linear-normal setting, several remarkable properties hold simultaneously: the posterior distribution is always normal, the MAP and LMS estimates always coincide, the estimates are always linear functions of the observations (of the form $`\hat{\theta} = a_0 + a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n`$), and the conditional MSE $`\text{var}(\Theta \mid X = x)`$ is the same for every value of $`x`$ (it depends only on the variances, not on the actual data observed).

### 2.7 Trajectory Estimation Example

A compelling application: estimating the trajectory of a free-falling object. The position at time $`t`$ is $`\Theta_0 + \Theta_1 t + \Theta_2 t^2`$, where $`\Theta_0`$ is the initial position, $`\Theta_1`$ is the initial velocity, and $`\Theta_2 = -9.81/2`$ (known gravity). We observe noisy measurements $`X_i = \Theta_0 + \Theta_1 t_i + \Theta_2 t_i^2 + W_i`$ at times $`t_1, \ldots, t_n`$.

With normal priors on $`\Theta_0`$ and $`\Theta_1`$ and normal noise $`W_i`$, the MAP estimate minimizes a sum of squared terms: the prior deviations $`(\Theta_0 - 200)^2/\sigma_0^2 + (\Theta_1 - 50)^2/\sigma_1^2`$ plus the data-fit terms $`\sum_i (X_i - \Theta_0 - \Theta_1 t_i - \Theta_2 t_i^2)^2 / \sigma_W^2`$. Setting partial derivatives to zero yields a linear system whose solution gives the MAP (= LMS) estimates of $`\Theta_0`$ and $`\Theta_1`$.

The posterior also gives 95% confidence intervals around the estimated trajectory, quantifying where the true trajectory is likely to lie.

### 2.8 General Linear Normal Models

More broadly, if $`\Theta_1, \ldots, \Theta_m`$ are independent normals and each observation $`X_i`$ is a linear combination of the $`\Theta_j`$ plus independent normal noise, then: the joint posterior of $`(\Theta_1, \ldots, \Theta_m)`$ given $`X = x`$ is multivariate normal, the MAP estimates are linear functions of the observations, the MAP and LMS estimates coincide, and the marginal posterior of each $`\Theta_j`$ is also normal.

## 3. Least Mean Squares (LMS) Estimation

### 3.1 LMS Without Observations

Before any data is available, the simplest estimation problem is: choose a constant $`\hat{\theta}`$ to minimize $`E[(\Theta - \hat{\theta})^2]`$. Taking the derivative and setting it to zero:

$$\frac{d}{d\hat{\theta}} E[(\Theta - \hat{\theta})^2] = -2E[\Theta - \hat{\theta}] = 0 \implies \hat{\theta} = E[\Theta]$$

The best constant estimate (in the MSE sense) is the prior mean. The corresponding MSE is $`\text{var}(\Theta)`$: without any data, the best we can do is guess the mean, and our error is the prior variance.

### 3.2 LMS With Observations

Now suppose we observe $`X = x`$. We want to choose $`\hat{\theta} = g(x)`$ to minimize the **conditional MSE** $`E[(\Theta - g(x))^2 \mid X = x]`$.

By the same logic as above (applied to the conditional distribution), the optimal estimate is:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{LMS}} = E[\Theta \mid X = x]$$

The **LMS estimator** $`\hat{\Theta} = E[\Theta \mid X]`$ (a function of the random observation $`X`$) minimizes the overall MSE $`E[(\Theta - g(X))^2]`$ over **all possible estimators** $`g(X)`$, with no restrictions on the functional form of $`g`$.

This is a fundamental result: the conditional expectation is the universally optimal estimator under squared error loss.

### 3.3 Performance of the LMS Estimator

The conditional MSE, once we have observed $`X = x`$, is:

$$E[(\Theta - \hat{\theta}_{\text{LMS}})^2 \mid X = x] = \text{var}(\Theta \mid X = x)$$

This is the variance of the posterior distribution. It tells us how much residual uncertainty remains about $`\Theta`$ after seeing the data.

The overall MSE is:

$$E[(\Theta - \hat{\Theta}_{\text{LMS}})^2] = E[\text{var}(\Theta \mid X)]$$

This is the average (over all possible observations) of the posterior variance.

### 3.4 Relation Between MAP and LMS

The LMS and MAP estimates coincide whenever the posterior distribution is **symmetric and unimodal** around its mean. This includes the important case of normal posteriors (which arise in all linear-normal models). When the posterior is skewed or multimodal, the MAP and LMS estimates generally differ.

### 3.5 Properties of the LMS Estimation Error

Define the estimation error $`\tilde{\Theta} = \Theta - \hat{\Theta}_{\text{LMS}} = \Theta - E[\Theta \mid X]`$. This error has several clean properties. The conditional expectation of the error given $`X`$ is zero: $`E[\tilde{\Theta} \mid X] = 0`$, meaning the estimator is unbiased for every realization of $`X`$. The overall expectation is also zero: $`E[\tilde{\Theta}] = 0`$. The error is uncorrelated with the estimate: $`\text{cov}(\tilde{\Theta}, \hat{\Theta}) = 0`$, which leads to the variance decomposition:

$$\text{var}(\Theta) = \text{var}(\hat{\Theta}) + \text{var}(\tilde{\Theta})$$

The total prior uncertainty about $`\Theta`$ is split into the variability of the estimate (signal captured) and the variability of the error (residual noise).

### 3.6 LMS With Multiple Observations

When we observe a vector $`X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)`$, the LMS estimate is simply:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{LMS}} = E[\Theta \mid X_1 = x_1, \ldots, X_n = x_n]$$

If there are multiple unknowns $`\Theta_1, \ldots, \Theta_m`$, each is estimated separately by its own conditional expectation given all observations.

### 3.7 Challenges

In practice, LMS estimation can be difficult because computing $`E[\Theta \mid X = x]`$ requires knowing the full posterior distribution, which in turn requires evaluating the Bayes rule integral. For non-normal, non-conjugate models, these integrals may not have closed-form solutions, motivating the approximate methods in the next section.

## 4. Linear Least Mean Squares (LLMS) Estimation

### 4.1 Motivation

The LMS estimator $`E[\Theta \mid X]`$ is optimal but can be hard to compute or implement. A practical alternative is to **restrict** the estimator to be a **linear function** of the observations: $`\hat{\Theta} = aX + b`$, and then choose $`a`$ and $`b`$ to minimize $`E[(\Theta - aX - b)^2]`$. This is the **Linear Least Mean Squares (LLMS)** estimator.

### 4.2 Derivation

We minimize $`E[(\Theta - aX - b)^2]`$ by taking partial derivatives with respect to $`a`$ and $`b`$ and setting them to zero.

**Optimizing over $`b`$** (for a fixed $`a`$): the optimal $`b`$ satisfies $`b = E[\Theta] - aE[X]`$. Substituting back, the problem reduces to minimizing $`\text{var}(\Theta - aX)`$ over $`a`$, which gives:

$$a = \frac{\text{cov}(\Theta, X)}{\text{var}(X)} = \rho \cdot \frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X}$$

where $`\rho = \rho(\Theta, X)`$ is the correlation coefficient.

### 4.3 The LLMS Estimator

$$\hat{\Theta}_{\text{LLMS}} = E[\Theta] + \frac{\text{cov}(\Theta, X)}{\text{var}(X)} \left(X - E[X]\right) = E[\Theta] + \rho \frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X} \left(X - E[X]\right)$$

The estimator starts at the prior mean $`E[\Theta]`$ and adjusts linearly based on how far $`X`$ deviates from its own mean. The adjustment is scaled by the correlation $`\rho`$ and the ratio of standard deviations.

### 4.4 Interpreting the LLMS Estimator

When $`\rho > 0`$ and we observe $`X > E[X]`$ (above-average observation), the estimate is pulled above $`E[\Theta]`$. When $`\rho = 0`$ ($`\Theta`$ and $`X`$ are uncorrelated), the observation provides no useful linear information, so $`\hat{\Theta}_{\text{LLMS}} = E[\Theta]`$, just the prior mean. When $`|\rho| = 1`$, the relationship is perfectly linear and the estimator is exact.

### 4.5 LLMS Mean Squared Error

$$E[(\hat{\Theta}_{\text{LLMS}} - \Theta)^2] = (1 - \rho^2) \cdot \text{var}(\Theta)$$

This shows that the fraction of variance "explained" by the linear estimator is $`\rho^2`$. If $`|\rho| = 1`$, the MSE is zero (perfect estimation). If $`\rho = 0`$, the MSE equals $`\text{var}(\Theta)`$ (no improvement over the prior).

### 4.6 Key Advantages of LLMS

LLMS only requires knowledge of $`E[\Theta]`$, $`E[X]`$, $`\text{var}(\Theta)`$, $`\text{var}(X)`$, and $`\text{cov}(\Theta, X)`$. You do not need the full joint distribution of $`(\Theta, X)`$. This makes LLMS much more practical than LMS in many real-world settings.

If $`E[\Theta \mid X]`$ happens to be a linear function of $`X`$ (as in the linear-normal model), then LLMS and LMS coincide: restricting to linear estimators costs nothing.

### 4.7 Example: Coin Bias with LLMS

Returning to the coin example: $`\Theta \sim \text{Uniform}[0,1]`$ (so $`E[\Theta] = 1/2`$, $`\text{var}(\Theta) = 1/12`$), and $`X \mid \Theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \Theta)`$. Through careful computation of $`E[X] = n/2`$, $`\text{var}(X) = n(n+2)/12`$, and $`\text{cov}(\Theta, X) = n/12`$, the LLMS estimator is:

$$\hat{\Theta}_{\text{LLMS}} = \frac{X + 1}{n + 2}$$

This is the same as the LMS estimate $`E[\Theta \mid X = k] = (k+1)/(n+2)`$, confirming that for this problem, the conditional expectation happens to be linear in $`X`$.

### 4.8 LLMS with Multiple Observations

With observations $`X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)`$, the LLMS estimator takes the form:

$$\hat{\Theta} = a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2 + \cdots + a_n X_n + b$$

The optimal coefficients are found by solving a system of $`n + 1`$ linear equations (the normal equations), which involve only the means, variances, and covariances of $`\Theta, X_1, \ldots, X_n`$.

If $`\Theta, X_1, \ldots, X_n`$ are jointly normal, then $`E[\Theta \mid X]`$ is automatically linear in $`X`$, so LLMS = LMS.

### 4.9 The Representation of the Data Matters

An important subtlety: the LMS estimator $`E[\Theta \mid X]`$ gives the same result whether you condition on $`X`$ or on any invertible transformation of $`X`$ (like $`X^3`$). But the LLMS estimator does depend on how you represent the data. Using $`\hat{\Theta} = aX + b`$ and $`\hat{\Theta} = aX^3 + b`$ generally give different answers, because linearity in $`X`$ and linearity in $`X^3`$ are different constraints. You can also consider richer models like $`\hat{\Theta} = a_1 X + a_2 e^X + a_3 \log X + b`$ to improve accuracy.

## 5. Inequalities, Convergence, and the Weak Law of Large Numbers

### 5.1 The Markov Inequality

Sometimes we cannot compute exact probabilities but want to **bound** the probability of extreme events. The Markov inequality gives a bound using only the mean.

**Markov inequality.** If $`X \ge 0`$ and $`a > 0`$, then:

$$P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}$$

The proof is elegant: $`E[X] = E[X \cdot \mathbf{1}_{X \ge a}] + E[X \cdot \mathbf{1}_{X < a}] \ge E[X \cdot \mathbf{1}_{X \ge a}] \ge a \cdot P(X \ge a)`$.

The Markov inequality is very general (it applies to any nonnegative random variable) but often quite loose. Its primary value is as a building block for stronger results.

**Example.** If $`X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda = 1)`$, then $`E[X] = 1`$, and the Markov inequality gives $`P(X \ge a) \le 1/a`$. The exact value is $`e^{-a}`$, which is much smaller for large $`a`$, showing that the bound is conservative.

### 5.2 The Chebyshev Inequality

The Chebyshev inequality uses both the mean and the variance to bound the probability of deviating from the mean.

**Chebyshev inequality.** For any random variable $`X`$ with finite mean $`\mu`$ and variance $`\sigma^2`$, and any $`k > 0`$:

$$P(|X - \mu| \ge k) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2}$$

This is derived by applying the Markov inequality to the nonnegative random variable $`(X - \mu)^2`$: $`P((X-\mu)^2 \ge k^2) \le E[(X-\mu)^2]/k^2 = \sigma^2/k^2`$.

An equivalent formulation: $`P(|X - \mu| \ge c\sigma) \le 1/c^2`$. So no matter what the distribution looks like, at least 75% of the probability lies within 2 standard deviations of the mean, and at least 89% within 3 standard deviations.

### 5.3 The Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

The WLLN formalizes the intuition that sample averages converge to the population mean. Let $`X_1, X_2, \ldots`$ be i.i.d. random variables with finite mean $`\mu`$ and variance $`\sigma^2`$. The sample mean is:

$$M_n = \frac{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}{n}$$

Then $`E[M_n] = \mu`$ and $`\text{var}(M_n) = \sigma^2/n`$. Applying the Chebyshev inequality:

$$P(|M_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}$$

For any fixed $`\epsilon > 0`$, the right side goes to zero as $`n \to \infty`$:

$$\text{WLLN: } P(|M_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \to 0 \text{ as } n \to \infty$$

**Interpretation.** In the context of a single experiment with many measurements $`X_i = \mu + W_i`$ (where $`W_i`$ is zero-mean noise), the sample mean $`M_n`$ concentrates around the true value $`\mu`$ as $`n`$ grows. In the context of many repetitions of the same experiment, the empirical frequency of an event converges to its probability. This is the precise mathematical statement behind the frequentist interpretation of probability.

### 5.4 The Pollster's Problem (Chebyshev Version)

Suppose a fraction $`p`$ of the population supports a referendum. A pollster samples $`n`$ people at random and computes $`M_n`$, the fraction of "yes" in the sample. We want $`P(|M_n - p| \ge 0.01) \le 0.05`$.

Since each $`X_i`$ is Bernoulli($`p`$), $`\sigma^2 = p(1-p) \le 1/4`$. Chebyshev gives:

$$P(|M_n - p| \ge 0.01) \le \frac{1/4}{n \cdot (0.01)^2} = \frac{2500}{n}$$

Setting this $`\le 0.05`$ requires $`n \ge 50{,}000`$. This is a conservative bound; the CLT (Section 6) gives a much tighter sample size requirement.

### 5.5 Convergence in Probability

The WLLN says $`M_n`$ "converges" to $`\mu`$, but we need to be precise about what convergence means for random variables.

**Definition.** A sequence of random variables $`Y_n`$ **converges in probability** to a number $`a`$ if for every $`\epsilon > 0`$:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|Y_n - a| \ge \epsilon) = 0$$

This means that the probability mass of $`Y_n`$ eventually concentrates near $`a`$, but there is no guarantee about individual realizations. In any single run, $`Y_n`$ might deviate from $`a`$, but the probability of such deviations becomes negligible.

**Properties.** If $`X_n \to a`$ and $`Y_n \to b`$ in probability, then: $`X_n + Y_n \to a + b`$, and $`g(X_n) \to g(a)`$ for any continuous function $`g`$.

**Caution.** Convergence in probability does **not** imply convergence of expectations. A sequence of random variables can converge to zero in probability while having expectations that diverge to infinity (if the distribution has a heavy enough tail).

### 5.6 Convergence With Probability 1 and the Strong Law

A stronger notion: $`Y_n`$ converges to $`a`$ **with probability 1** (almost surely) if $`P(\lbrace \omega : Y_n(\omega) \to a \rbrace) = 1`$. The **Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)** says $`M_n \to \mu`$ with probability 1, which is a strictly stronger statement than the WLLN.

## 6. The Central Limit Theorem

### 6.1 Different Scalings of the Sum

Let $`X_1, \ldots, X_n`$ be i.i.d. with mean $`\mu`$ and variance $`\sigma^2`$, and let $`S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n`$.

The raw sum $`S_n`$ has mean $`n\mu`$ and variance $`n\sigma^2`$, both growing with $`n`$, so $`S_n`$ does not converge to anything.

The sample mean $`M_n = S_n/n`$ has mean $`\mu`$ and variance $`\sigma^2/n \to 0`$, so $`M_n`$ converges to the constant $`\mu`$ (the WLLN).

The interesting scaling is the one that keeps the variance constant:

$$Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} = \frac{M_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

This has $`E[Z_n] = 0`$ and $`\text{var}(Z_n) = 1`$ for every $`n`$.

### 6.2 Statement of the CLT

**Central Limit Theorem.** For every $`z`$:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(Z_n \le z) = \Phi(z)$$

where $`\Phi(z)`$ is the CDF of the standard normal distribution $`N(0,1)`$.

In practice, we use this as an approximation for finite $`n`$: treat $`S_n`$ as approximately $`N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)`$, or equivalently, treat $`Z_n`$ as approximately $`N(0,1)`$.

### 6.3 Why the CLT Is So Powerful

The CLT is **universal**: it holds regardless of the distribution of the individual $`X_i`$, as long as they have finite mean and variance. This is what justifies using the normal distribution so pervasively. Whenever a quantity is the sum of many small, independent contributions, the CLT tells us it will be approximately normal, regardless of how the individual contributions are distributed.

Practically, the CLT is a computational shortcut: to evaluate $`P(S_n \le a)`$, you just standardize and look up the standard normal table. No need to compute $`n`$-fold convolutions.

The approximation tends to be quite accurate even for moderate $`n`$ (often $`n \ge 30`$ suffices). Symmetry and unimodality of the underlying distribution help; heavy tails and strong skewness require larger $`n`$.

### 6.4 Example: Sum of Exponentials

Package weights $`X_i`$ are i.i.d. $`\text{Exponential}(\lambda = 1/2)`$, so $`\mu = 2`$ and $`\sigma = 2`$. Load $`n = 100`$ packages into a container.

$$P(S_{100} > 210) = P\left(Z_{100} > \frac{210 - 200}{20}\right) = P(Z > 0.5) = 1 - \Phi(0.5) \approx 0.3085$$

**Inverse problems.** Given $`n = 100`$, find capacity $`a`$ such that $`P(S_n > a) \approx 0.05`$: we need $`(a - 200)/20 = 1.645`$, giving $`a = 232.9`$. Given the capacity $`a = 210`$, find the maximum $`n`$ such that $`P(S_n > 210) \le 0.05`$: solve $`(210 - 2n)/(2\sqrt{n}) = 1.645`$, giving $`n \approx 89`$.

### 6.5 Normal Approximation to the Binomial

If $`S_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)`$, the CLT says $`S_n`$ is approximately $`N(np, np(1-p))`$. For example, with $`n = 36`$ and $`p = 0.5`$:

$$P(S_{36} \le 21) = P\left(Z \le \frac{21 - 18}{3}\right) = \Phi(1) \approx 0.8413$$

The exact value is 0.8785. The discrepancy arises because $`S_n`$ is integer-valued while the normal is continuous.

### 6.6 The Continuity Correction

To improve the normal approximation for integer-valued random variables, use the **continuity correction** (also called the De Moivre-Laplace correction):

$$P(S_n \le k) \approx \Phi\left(\frac{k + 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)$$

For the example above: $`\Phi((21.5 - 18)/3) = \Phi(1.17) \approx 0.8790`$, which is much closer to the exact answer 0.8785.

The continuity correction also allows the CLT to approximate individual PMF values: $`P(S_n = k) \approx \Phi((k+0.5 - np)/\sigma) - \Phi((k-0.5 - np)/\sigma)`$.

### 6.7 The Pollster's Problem (CLT Version)

Returning to the polling example with $`P(|M_n - p| \ge 0.01) \le 0.05`$. Using the CLT:

$$P(|M_n - p| \ge 0.01) = P\left(|Z| \ge \frac{0.01\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}\right)$$

where $`\sigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)} \le 1/2`$. We need $`0.01\sqrt{n}/0.5 \ge 1.96`$ (since $`P(|Z| \ge 1.96) \approx 0.05`$). This gives $`\sqrt{n} \ge 98`$, i.e., $`n \ge 9{,}604`$. Compared to the Chebyshev bound of $`n \ge 50{,}000`$, the CLT gives a much more practical sample size. With $`n = 10{,}000`$: $`P(|M_n - p| \ge 0.01) \approx 2(1 - \Phi(2)) \approx 0.046 < 0.05`$.

## 7. Introduction to Classical Statistics

### 7.1 Classical vs. Bayesian

In **Bayesian** inference, the unknown $`\Theta`$ is a random variable with a prior, and we compute the posterior $`p_{\Theta \mid X}`$ using Bayes' rule. In **classical (frequentist)** statistics, the unknown $`\theta`$ is a **fixed but unknown constant** (not a random variable). There is no prior distribution, no posterior distribution, and no Bayes' rule.

Instead, we have a family of models $`p_X(x; \theta)`$ indexed by $`\theta`$, and we design an estimator $`\hat{\Theta}_n = g(X_1, \ldots, X_n)`$ based on $`n`$ data points. The notation $`p_X(x; \theta)`$ is deliberately not written as $`p_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)`$ because $`\theta`$ is not a random variable: it does not have a probability distribution.

### 7.2 Estimating a Mean

The most fundamental classical problem: $`X_1, \ldots, X_n`$ are i.i.d. with mean $`\theta`$ and variance $`\sigma^2`$. The natural estimator is the sample mean:

$$\hat{\Theta}_n = M_n = \frac{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}{n}$$

**Properties and terminology:**

The estimator is **unbiased**: $`E[\hat{\Theta}_n] = \theta`$ for all $`\theta`$. This means that on average, across many repetitions of the experiment, the estimator hits the true value.

The estimator is **consistent**: $`\hat{\Theta}_n \to \theta`$ in probability as $`n \to \infty`$ (by the WLLN). With enough data, the estimator converges to the truth.

The **mean squared error** of any estimator decomposes as:

$$\text{MSE} = E[(\hat{\Theta} - \theta)^2] = \text{var}(\hat{\Theta}) + (\text{bias})^2$$

For the sample mean, $`\text{bias} = 0`$ and $`\text{var}(\hat{\Theta}_n) = \sigma^2/n`$, so $`\text{MSE} = \sigma^2/n`$.

The quantity $`\sqrt{\text{var}(\hat{\Theta})}`$ is called the **standard error** of the estimator.

### 7.3 Confidence Intervals

A point estimate alone does not convey how uncertain it is. A **confidence interval** provides a range of plausible values for $`\theta`$.

**Definition.** An interval $`[\hat{\Theta}^-, \hat{\Theta}^+]`$ (computed from the data) is a $`(1 - \alpha)`$ **confidence interval** for $`\theta`$ if:

$$P(\hat{\Theta}^- \le \theta \le \hat{\Theta}^+) \ge 1 - \alpha \quad \text{for all } \theta$$

Common choices: $`\alpha = 0.05`$ (95% CI), $`\alpha = 0.01`$ (99% CI).

**Interpretation.** The confidence level does not say there is a 95% probability that $`\theta`$ lies in the computed interval. ($`\theta`$ is a fixed constant; it either lies in the interval or it does not.) Rather, if you repeated the experiment many times and computed a 95% CI each time, approximately 95% of those intervals would contain $`\theta`$.

### 7.4 Confidence Interval for the Mean Using the CLT

By the CLT, $`Z_n = (\hat{\Theta}_n - \theta)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})`$ is approximately $`N(0,1)`$. Since $`\Phi(1.96) \approx 0.975`$:

$$P\left(\hat{\Theta}_n - \frac{1.96\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \theta \le \hat{\Theta}_n + \frac{1.96\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \approx 0.95$$

So the 95% confidence interval is:

$$\left[\hat{\Theta}_n - \frac{1.96\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \;\; \hat{\Theta}_n + \frac{1.96\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

### 7.5 When $`\sigma`$ Is Unknown

In practice, $`\sigma`$ is often unknown. Three approaches:

**Upper bound.** If we know that $`\sigma \le \sigma_{\max}`$ (e.g., for Bernoulli random variables, $`\sigma \le 1/2`$), use $`\sigma_{\max}`$ in the formula. This gives a valid but wider CI.

**Plug-in estimate.** Use the sample standard deviation $`\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \hat{\Theta}_n)^2}`$ or the Bessel-corrected version $`\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \hat{\Theta}_n)^2}`$ in place of $`\sigma`$. For Bernoulli data, $`\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\hat{\Theta}_n(1 - \hat{\Theta}_n)}`$.

**t-distribution.** For small $`n`$ (say $`n < 30`$), the plug-in approach introduces additional uncertainty. The correct adjustment replaces the normal quantile 1.96 with the slightly larger $`t`$-distribution quantile, which accounts for the estimation of $`\sigma`$. As $`n \to \infty`$, the $`t`$-quantile converges to the normal quantile.

### 7.6 Other Natural Estimators

The sample mean is an estimator of $`E[X]`$, but we can estimate any quantity that can be expressed as an expectation. If $`\theta = E[g(X)]`$, the natural estimator is:

$$\hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} g(X_i)$$

This covers: the sample variance $`\hat{v}_X = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \hat{\theta}_X)^2`$ as an estimator of $`\text{var}(X)`$, the sample covariance $`\widehat{\text{cov}}(X,Y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \hat{\theta}_X)(Y_i - \hat{\theta}_Y)`$ as an estimator of $`\text{cov}(X,Y)`$, and the sample correlation $`\hat{\rho} = \widehat{\text{cov}}(X,Y)/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_X}\sqrt{\hat{v}_Y})`$ as an estimator of $`\rho(X,Y)`$.

### 7.7 Maximum Likelihood (ML) Estimation

**Maximum Likelihood** is a general-purpose method for constructing estimators. The idea: pick the value of $`\theta`$ that makes the observed data as probable as possible.

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta \, p_X(x; \theta)$$

For continuous data: $`\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta \, f_X(x; \theta)`$.

**Comparison to MAP.** The MAP estimator maximizes $`f_\Theta(\theta) \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)`$ (prior times likelihood). The ML estimator maximizes $`f_X(x; \theta)`$ (likelihood only). If the Bayesian prior is uniform (flat), the MAP and ML estimates coincide.

### 7.8 Properties of ML Estimators

Under mild regularity conditions and with $`n`$ i.i.d. observations, the ML estimator is: **consistent** ($`\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta`$ in probability), **asymptotically normal** ($`(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta)/\text{se}(\hat{\theta}_n) \to N(0,1)`$ in distribution), and **asymptotically efficient** (achieves the smallest possible variance among all consistent estimators, in a precise sense called the Cramer-Rao bound).

These properties make ML the default choice in classical statistics when no special structure is available.

### 7.9 ML Example: Binomial Parameter

$`K \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)`$ where $`n`$ is known and $`\theta`$ is unknown. The likelihood is $`\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}`$. Maximizing over $`\theta`$:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \frac{k}{n}$$

This is the same as the MAP estimator with a uniform prior on $`\theta`$, confirming the connection.

### 7.10 ML Example: Normal Mean and Variance

$`X_1, \ldots, X_n`$ i.i.d. $`N(\mu, v)`$ where both $`\mu`$ and $`v`$ are unknown. The log-likelihood is:

$$\ell(\mu, v) = -\frac{n}{2}\log v - \frac{1}{2v}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2 + \text{const}$$

Maximizing over $`\mu`$: $`\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i`$ (the sample mean).

Maximizing over $`v`$: $`\hat{v} = \frac{1}{n}\sum (x_i - \hat{\mu})^2`$ (the sample variance with $`n`$ in the denominator, not $`n-1`$).

Note that the ML estimator of the variance uses $`1/n`$ and is slightly biased downward. The unbiased version uses $`1/(n-1)`$ (Bessel's correction).

## Quick Reference: Key Formulas

**Bayes' rule (continuous-continuous):**\
$$f_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x) = \frac{f_\Theta(\theta) \cdot f_{X \mid \Theta}(x \mid \theta)}{f_X(x)}$$

**Linear-normal posterior (multiple observations):**\
$$E[\Theta \mid X = x] = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n} x_i/\sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=0}^{n} 1/\sigma_i^2}, \qquad \text{var}(\Theta \mid X) = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{n} 1/\sigma_i^2}$$

**LMS estimator:**\
$$\hat{\Theta}_{\text{LMS}} = E[\Theta \mid X], \qquad \text{MSE} = E[\text{var}(\Theta \mid X)]$$

**LLMS estimator:**\
$$\hat{\Theta}_{\text{LLMS}} = E[\Theta] + \rho \frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X}(X - E[X]), \qquad \text{MSE} = (1 - \rho^2)\,\text{var}(\Theta)$$

**Markov inequality:**\
$$P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a} \quad (X \ge 0, \; a > 0)$$

**Chebyshev inequality:**\
$$P(|X - \mu| \ge k) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2}$$

**Weak Law of Large Numbers:**\
$$P(|M_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \to 0 \text{ as } n \to \infty$$

**Central Limit Theorem:**\
$$Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)$$

**95% Confidence Interval for the mean:**\
$$\left[\hat{\Theta}_n - \frac{1.96\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \;\; \hat{\Theta}_n + \frac{1.96\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

**Maximum Likelihood estimator:**

$$
\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \operatorname{argmax}_\theta \, p_X(x; \theta)
$$

**MSE decomposition (classical):**\
$$\text{MSE} = \text{var}(\hat{\Theta}) + (\text{bias})^2$$
